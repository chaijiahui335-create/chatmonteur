# Spoken-Text Layout for Chinese Talking-Head Videos

## Critical distinction

Spoken-text layout is NOT automatic subtitle transcription.

A transcript answers: "What words were spoken, and when?"
A spoken-text layout answers: "Which part of what was spoken should become the visual text, how should it be arranged, and what should the viewer notice?"

The final video may use the complete spoken sentence as readable captions, but the visual treatment must be editorially designed around meaning, hierarchy, rhythm, and emphasis.

## Three separate text layers

### 1. Spoken caption layer

Represents the actual spoken content faithfully enough for comprehension.

- Segment by Chinese phrase and meaning.
- Do not blindly copy ASR segmentation.
- Merge fragments split incorrectly by ASR.
- Correct obvious recognition errors only when context is reliable.
- Preserve the speaker's wording; do not rewrite claims.
- Keep the caption visually subordinate to designed emphasis text.

### 2. Editorial emphasis layer

Extract the most important phrase from the spoken sentence.

Typical length: 2–10 Chinese characters, occasionally longer when a complete phrase is necessary.

Examples of emphasis targets:

- problem: "真正的问题"
- contrast: "不是产品不好"
- conclusion: "关键是不会讲"
- number: "3个细节"
- warning: "别急着降价"
- benefit: "客户一眼就懂"

The emphasis phrase must be supported by what the speaker actually says. Never invent a stronger claim.

### 3. Context/title layer

Used sparingly for section changes, hooks, comparisons, examples, or conclusions. It is not a copy of the subtitle sentence.

## Layout principles

- Treat each spoken sentence as a small visual composition.
- Use line breaks deliberately for Chinese reading rhythm.
- Prefer asymmetric composition when appropriate rather than centered text on every shot.
- Create hierarchy through size, weight, spacing, position, and selective emphasis.
- Keep the speaker's eyes, mouth, hands, and product unobstructed.
- Design for 9:16 mobile viewing.
- Do not use a permanent subtitle bar unless the project explicitly requires it.
- Avoid rainbow text, excessive outlines, bouncing words, and decorative karaoke effects.

## Visual timing

Text changes should follow semantic beats, not a fixed character-per-second formula.

When a sentence contains a contrast:

1. Show the setup in normal caption treatment.
2. Introduce the first side of the contrast.
3. Change composition or emphasis at the contrast word.
4. Land on the key phrase with a stronger text treatment.

When a sentence contains a concrete object/product:

1. Keep the spoken caption readable.
2. Bring the product image/video in at the spoken anchor.
3. Use a short editorial label or callout for the relevant detail.
4. Return focus to the speaker when the visual explanation is complete.

## Text should interact with the picture

The text layer can become part of the composition:

- headline beside the speaker
- emphasis phrase above/beside a product
- short label pointing to a product detail
- large phrase behind a cutout speaker when contrast is needed
- split-screen text + evidence image
- numbered points synchronized to spoken points

Text must never merely sit at the bottom because "subtitles go there."

## Anti-patterns

Reject these as insufficient:

- ASR transcript pasted unchanged onto the screen
- every word highlighted in sequence
- every sentence centered at the bottom
- identical font size and line structure throughout
- full-screen text replacing the speaker without editorial reason
- large decorative words that do not correspond to speech
- text covering the product or facial expression

## QC question

Before delivery, ask:

"If I hide the audio, do the designed text elements still tell me what the speaker is emphasizing?"

If the answer is no, the edit needs editorial text design rather than more subtitle styling.
